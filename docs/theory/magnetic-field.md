# Magnetic field

*Built, in active development, not approved yet* (roadmap O18 step 4,
first slice). Implemented in `crates/ohl-electrical`
(`magnetic`). Used by `ohl electrical magnetic` and Checks › Electrical ›
Magnetic field… ([Magnetic field](../user-guide/magnetic-field.md): the
window step by step, every setting; [Electrical: magnetic
field](../user-guide/analyses.md#electrical-magnetic-field): the command
line).

Every assumption is marked **Assumption** (a value or a model taken where
the line gives none) or **Simplification** (it changes the result), and
said to be **conservative** (the field computed is at least the real one),
**not conservative**, or neither, as far as that can be told. The program
computes the field; **which current, which places and which limit** an
ordinance asks for are the ordinance's (a plugin's parameter file), never
built in.

## What is computed

The **magnetic flux density** of the line's currents at power frequency,
as the rms value of the vector (the *resultant* field):

\\[ B = \sqrt{|\underline B_x|^2 + |\underline B_y|^2 + |\underline B_z|^2} \\]

with \\( \underline B_x, \underline B_y, \underline B_z \\) the complex
rms phasors of the three components. The phases between the components do
not enter (the rms of the vector, not its peak). This is the quantity the
Swiss enforcement guide asks for (BAFU, *Hochspannungsleitungen.
Vollzugshilfe zur NISV*, draft for trial, 2007, section 8.1.4, its
equation 2) and the "resultant field" of IEC 62110 (*Electric and
magnetic field levels generated by AC power systems — Measurement
procedures with regard to public exposure*, 2009).

Beside it the program gives the **largest value over the cycle**: the
field vector \\( \Re(\vec{\underline B}\,e^{j\omega t}) \\) traces an
ellipse; with \\( \vec{\underline B} = \vec R + j\vec I \\) its semi-major
axis (as an rms value) is

\\[ B_{max} = \sqrt{\tfrac12(R^2 + I^2) + \sqrt{\tfrac14(R^2 - I^2)^2 + (\vec R\cdot\vec I)^2}} \\]

(the largest eigenvalue of \\( \vec R\vec R^T + \vec I\vec I^T \\)).
\\( B_{max} \le B \le \sqrt2\,B_{max} \\): equal for a linearly
polarised field, \\( B/\sqrt2 \\) for a circular one.

**Several frequencies** (a 16.7 Hz railway system beside a 50 Hz one):
each frequency computed apart, added as
\\( B = \sqrt{B_{16.7}^2 + B_{50}^2} \\) (Parseval; the guide's equation
3). The largest value over the cycle is then given as the root of the
sum of each frequency's squares — not a physical value (two frequencies
have no common cycle), only an indication.

![Left: the three phase currents as phasors at 0, −120 and +120 degrees. Middle: at a point every wire adds its field per component as a phasor; B is the rms of the vector. Right: the field vector R + jI traces an ellipse over a cycle; its semi-major axis is B_max](../images/theory/m-phasors.svg)

**What it is compared to.** A limit (µT) is optional and yours to give:
each point then shows its margin (limit − B) and each profile the width
where B is at or above it. No limit is built in. Which current, which
places and which load-flow directions govern are the ordinance's (a
plugin's parameter file).

## How it works, step by step

1. **The wires.** The line is solved in the weather case: each wire's
   polyline per span, a bundle subconductor by subconductor, each
   jumper's shape.
2. **The currents.** Each circuit's current (given, from its end points,
   or its thermal rating) at its phase angles, each section's direction
   along its conductor path ([Currents](#currents), [Which way the
   currents flow](#which-way-the-currents-flow)); optionally the earth
   wires' and structures' currents ([Earth wire
   currents](#earth-wire-currents)).
3. **The field.** At every point, every straight piece of every wire
   adds its field in closed form (Biot–Savart), per component as a
   phasor ([The 3D model](#the-3d-model)); then \\( B \\) and
   \\( B_{max} \\) as above. No ground: the earth's currents are left
   out ([The ground's return currents](#the-grounds-return-currents--left-out)).
4. **The views.** Profiles are plotted; grids, cuts and volumes are drawn
   between their points, and how far the drawing lies off its level is
   measured ([Cuts, volumes and what they are drawn
   with](#cuts-volumes-and-what-they-are-drawn-with)).

## The 3D model

Each conductor and earth wire as the solver gives it in the chosen
weather case — the polyline of every span (sag, line angles, the finite
length of the line as modelled), a bundle **subconductor by subconductor**
(each subconductor's polyline offset from the bundle's centre line as the
views draw it, `Bundle::polylines`). Each straight piece from \\( a \\) to
\\( b \\) carries a current \\( \underline I \\) (rms phasor); its field at
\\( p \\) in closed form (Biot–Savart's law integrated along the piece;
e.g. D. J. Griffiths, *Introduction to Electrodynamics*, the field of a
straight wire segment):

\\[ \vec B = \frac{\mu_0 \underline I}{4\pi\rho^2}
    \left( \frac{\vec r_a\cdot\hat u}{|\vec r_a|} - \frac{\vec r_b\cdot\hat u}{|\vec r_b|} \right)
    \hat u \times \vec d \\]

with \\( \hat u \\) the piece's direction, \\( \vec r_a = p - a \\),
\\( \vec r_b = p - b \\), \\( \vec d \\) the perpendicular from the piece's
line to \\( p \\), \\( \rho = |\vec d| \\) — the textbook
\\( \frac{\mu_0 I}{4\pi\rho}(\cos\alpha_1 - \cos\alpha_2) \\) as a vector.
The pieces' fields are summed per component as phasors.

![A straight piece from a to b carrying the current I and a point p beside it: the direction u, the perpendicular d of length rho, the vectors r_a and r_b and the angles alpha_1 and alpha_2; the field at p perpendicular to the plane of the piece and p](../images/theory/bs-segment.svg)

- **Simplification**: the wire straight between the solver's points. The
  solver says how far the wire lies off its polyline
  (`SpanShape.polyline_error`: the largest sagitta
  \\( R - \sqrt{R^2 - L^2/4} \approx L^2/(8R) \\) of a piece of length
  \\( L \\) on the catenary's smallest radius of curvature \\( R = H/w \\);
  some millimetres: 64 pieces per span or part of a span, e.g. 7 m pieces
  and 4 mm on a 450 m span at H/w = 1700 m); the field moves by about that
  share of the distance (0.04 % at 10 m). Neither conservative nor not.
  **Shorter pieces** on request (`Settings.piece_length`): each
  solver piece cut into equal parts no longer than asked, the new points
  on the average of the two quadratics through the piece's ends and a
  neighbouring point (parameter: the length along the polyline); half the
  two quadratics' difference is the placing's estimate, reported with the
  sagitta bound over the shorter pieces (\\( \propto L^2 \\)). Where the two
  differ by more than the solver's bound (a kink: a spacer, a clamp, a
  span point) the piece is cut on its chord — no worse than the solver's
  pieces there. On a catenary of \\( C \\) = 1500 m, 6.25 m pieces cut to
  0.9 m put the points within some µm of the curve (the chords lay up to
  3.3 mm off).
- **The sum** is the same for every piece; it is arranged per polyline
  (each vertex's distance computed once for the two pieces meeting there)
  and per block of 64 points (a wire's pieces stay in the processor's
  cache for the block): equal to the piece-by-piece sum within rounding
  (\\( 10^{-12} \\), tested), some times quicker on a long line.
- **Assumption**: a bundle's current shared equally by its
  subconductors (exact for a regular bundle by symmetry, close for the
  others).
- **Assumption**: the permeability of free space everywhere — the steel
  of the structures and of the earth wires' cores is not modelled
  magnetically (the structures' **currents** are: see *Earth wire
  currents*). Its size: a steel member of radius \\( a \\) and relative
  permeability \\( \mu_r \\) in a field across it disturbs that field
  outside it by at most \\( \frac{\mu_r - 1}{\mu_r + 1} (a/r)^2 \\) at
  the distance \\( r \\) from its axis (the 2D cylinder in a uniform field,
  exact): for a leg of 5 cm under 0.25 % at 1 m and 0.01 % at 5 m, for
  any \\( \mu_r \\). A lattice tower adds many such small disturbances
  near it; at mid-span and at the profiles' height none. Modelling it
  would take a finite-element field solution of the tower.
- **The jumpers** (since 2026-10-29; on by default, an option): a jumper
  between two section ends of one phase carries that phase's current as
  solved in the weather case, from the end whose section brings the
  current into the structure (by the sections' directions along the line
  and the circuit's load flow) to the end whose section takes it on. A
  jumper on a tap (the current shares itself), between different phases
  or between two ends the current enters (or leaves) by is left out and
  said (**not conservative** near that structure). Tested: a line cut at
  a structure and jumpered gives within 2.5 % the field of the line uncut
  right beside it, 9–10 % less without the jumper. Off: left out (**not
  conservative** near the structures).
- **The modelled length.** The line is modelled whole, as far as it goes.
  The guide asks for every conductor to be modelled at least five times
  the limit line's largest distance from a conductor before and after the
  plane of calculation (8.1.4.1). The program measures that reach on the
  profile (the farthest point at or above the limit from the nearest
  conductor at the station; without a limit, the profile's half width) and
  **warns** where the line is shorter than five times it on either side —
  near the line's ends, where the lines beyond (a substation, the next
  line) are not modelled: **not conservative** there. The reach is
  measured at the profile's height only; the limit line may reach farther
  at the conductors' height (**not conservative** for the rule's check).

## The 2D model

The wires of a cross-section as **infinite straight parallel
conductors**; per conductor

\\[ \vec B = \frac{\mu_0 \underline I}{2\pi r^2}\,\hat x \times \vec r, \qquad |\vec B| = \frac{\mu_0 |\underline I|}{2\pi r}. \\]

The wires' places: by default **where they cross the profile's plane**
(the cross-section at the station — at the span's lowest point by default:
the wires lowest, the field there largest). For a sagging span this is
**conservative** against the 3D model (the real wires rise towards the
structures; on the synthetic example's 300 m spans the 2D maximum under the lowest point
is 0.1 % above the 3D one, more on long spans with a large sag). Option: the span's **mean height**
(the line parameters' rule) — **not conservative** under the lowest point.

The guide says when 2D is not to be used (8.1.4.2); the program warns:

- **within reach of a line angle** over 5° (a deviation from the straight
  line of more than 5°, the guide's footnote 27): the angle between the
  spans' axes at each structure; a structure within the profile's half
  width (or the limit line's reach, if larger) of the station is said;
- **on a span between differently arranged structures**: a wire's place
  relative to the others (across and up, relative to the wires' centre)
  differs by more than **0.5 m** between the span's two ends (a choice of
  the program, to confirm);
- **near crossings** with other lines: not checked — other lines are not
  in the model yet (said).

## Currents

Per **circuit** (the phases of one system in one named circuit): an rms
current, the same in each phase (**balanced**), **no harmonics**, at the
system's phase angles (L1 0°, L2 −120°, L3 +120° unless the system gives
its own; a transformer's fixed shift, e.g. Yd11, is typed as the system's
angles). This is the governing state the guide describes (2.4.1 and
8.1.4); real operation is unbalanced — **Assumption** as the guide asks,
neither conservative nor not in general.

- **At its end points**: a circuit's end points are the structures
  where its sections end without going on to another of them (each
  phase's sections joined where they meet, by its jumpers); at each the
  current into the line (out: negative; one end may take the rest).
  Every section's current then follows by **Kirchhoff's current law**:
  on each phase's sections, a section's current is the sum of the
  currents in on its first structure's side once it is taken away —
  exact for a line with branches; a **meshed** circuit (its sections
  closing a loop) needs its impedances (the load flow, roadmap O18 step
  2) and is refused. The direction of each section's current comes with
  it. Tested: in at one end and out at the other is the circuit's current
  along the line (in at the other: against it); a sum not zero, a
  structure not an end and two "rests" are refused.
- **Given** (A) for the whole circuit, or
- the circuit's **thermal rating** (`--thermal` on the command line): the
  steady-state ampacity of each section's wire ([Thermal
  rating](thermal.md)) times its subconductors, at a conductor
  temperature and in an ambient case **you give** — the least over the
  circuit's sections (the weakest section governs, as the guide's 2.4.1
  says for a line of mixed conductors). The guide's governing current is
  the thermal limit current at the ambient conditions it names (2.4.1);
  those conditions belong to the ordinance's parameter file, not to the
  program.

- **Per phase** (since 2026-11-11): each phase's current
  \\( \underline I_k = I_k \angle \varphi_k \\) typed — magnitude and
  angle, in the frame of the systems' phase angles, along the line. A
  circuit given so needs all its phases; it takes them in place of the
  balanced current (not together with the end points). The field is the
  same sum over the wires as before, each wire with its own phasor.

### Balanced input and the net current

**NOT ON THE SAFE SIDE far from the line.** A balanced circuit's
currents sum to zero: seen from far away its wires cancel, and its field
falls off as \\( 1/d^2 \\) with the distance \\( d \\). An unbalanced
circuit leaves a **net current**

\\[ \underline I_n = \sum_k \underline I_k \neq 0 , \\]

whose field is that of one conductor, falling off only as \\( 1/d \\).
For a flat circuit of phase spacing \\( s \\) (the far-field forms used
[below](#the-grounds-return-currents--left-out) for the earth's images),

\\[ B_{\text{bal}} \approx \frac{\sqrt3\,\mu_0\,I\,s}{2\pi\,d^2}, \qquad
    B_{\text{net}} = \frac{\mu_0\,I_n}{2\pi\,d}, \qquad
    B_{\text{net}} = B_{\text{bal}} \ \text{at} \ d^* = \frac{\sqrt3\,I\,s}{I_n} . \\]

Beyond \\( d^* \\) the net current governs. Computed for \\( I = 1000 \\) A
and \\( s = 8 \\) m: a net current of 5 % (50 A) governs beyond 277 m
(0.036 µT there), of 2 % (20 A) beyond 693 m (0.0058 µT), of 1 % (10 A)
beyond 1386 m (0.0014 µT). Balanced input holds no net current, so it
gives too little there — small values, which matter where a low limit or
a long limit line is asked. Every result with a balanced circuit says so
("NOT ON THE SAFE SIDE far from the line: …", `magnetic::BALANCED_NOTE`);
a result whose circuits are all given per phase names each circuit's net
current instead. Which unbalance to assume is the user's (none is built
in). Tested (`currents_per_phase_and_the_net_current`): the phases typed
at the balanced values give the balanced field to \\( 10^{-9} \\); three
in-phase currents of 100 A give \\( \mu_0 \cdot 300 / (2\pi \cdot 500) =
0.12 \\) µT at 500 m (within 0.5 %) and half of it at twice the distance,
where the balanced circuit gives a quarter; 5 % more current in one
phase of 1000 A gives more than twice the balanced field at 500 m.

A **direction** per circuit: the load flow along the line (from its first
structure to its last) or against it — every phase's current turned by
180°. Which combination of directions governs is the ordinance's question
(the guide's 2.4.2); the program computes the one given.

DC systems are left out (a static field; said).

## Which way the currents flow

A circuit's current flows on through its sections and the jumpers
joining them: the sections joined by jumpers are oriented
alike (a jumper from one's last point to another's first: the same way;
last to last or first to first: opposite ways), and each such chain is
turned so that it runs along the line over most of its length (span by
span, from the structure placed first along the alignments to the next;
off the alignments, the way most sections between the two structures are
drawn). "Reverse" on a circuit turns all its phases. Until 2026-10-29 each
span took its direction alone; where the order of a span's two structures
turned at a structure, a current left that structure by both sections
(on a real line 3 of 77 profiles moved by more than 1 %, one by 9.7 %),
and a section drawn backwards off the alignments made a span of its own.
Tested: a section given backwards gives the same field.

## Earth wire currents

Optional. The earth wires are taken as **infinitely long and earthed
everywhere** (zero voltage along them), so per span, from its mean
cross-section and the primitive series impedances of the [line
parameters](line-parameters.md) (Carson's earth return, the line's
method and soil resistivity):

\\[ \underline I_e = -\,Z_{ee}^{-1} Z_{ep}\,\underline I_p . \\]

**Simplification**: the towers' earthing (footing resistances) and the
earth wires' ends are not modelled; near the ends of a line and with high
footing resistances the real currents are smaller. Hence, since
2026-10-29,
**through the structures' earthing**:

![Four structures and three spans: the earth wire bonded at every structure at its voltage V_n, each structure leading V_n/R_n into the ground; in each span the earth wire current I_e driven by the phases and the voltage difference; Kirchhoff's current law at every structure](../images/theory/m-earth-network.svg)

- Every structure bonds its earth wires (one voltage \\( V_n \\)) and leads
  current into the ground through its **earthing resistance** \\( R_n \\)
  (its own, `Structure.earthing_resistance_ohm`; else the line's; else
  10 Ω **assumed**, said — a common design target for towers, measured
  values ranging from under 1 Ω to hundreds).
- In every span of length \\( L \\) from structure \\( a \\) to \\( b \\), the
  earth wires carry
  \\[ \underline I_e = (Z_{ee} L)^{-1} \big( (V_a - V_b)\,\mathbf 1 - Z_{ep} \underline I_p L \big) \\]
  (the same primitive matrices from the span's mean cross-section).
- Kirchhoff's current law at every structure,
  \\( \sum_{\text{spans}} \mathbf 1^T \underline I_e \text{ (out)} + V_n/R_n = 0 \\),
  is one linear system over the whole line (branches included); its
  solution gives the earth wires' currents per span and every
  structure's current into the ground \\( V_n / R_n \\). With every
  \\( R_n \to 0 \\) it is the infinitely long earth wire above (tested
  within 0.1 %); with very high ones no current flows; the structures'
  currents add to nothing (tested).
- **The structures' currents are current paths** of the 3D model: down
  the structure's axis from its highest earth wire point to its foot.
  **Simplification**: the current shares itself among the legs and
  members; the field within a few metres of the structure differs.
- **Simplification, not conservative**: the earth wires end at the
  line's end structures, earthed there alone; a substation's earthing
  grid (far lower) is not modelled — near the line's ends the real
  currents are larger.

On the synthetic double circuit (600 m, three structures of 10 Ω, 1000 A
per circuit in the same direction) the network gives 2.3 A in the earth
wire, the infinitely long earth wire 94 A: on a short line with real
footings the infinite assumption overstates them by far. The guide does not ask
for earth wire currents but takes them when the program computes them
(its footnote 24). Their effect has **either sign**: in the synthetic
double-circuit example (L1 lowest, L3 next to the earth wire) the induced
current is nearly in phase with L1 and **raises** the field near the
ground by about 5 % (one circuit) to 6 % (both); other arrangements lower it. Run both when it
matters.

### An earth wire's current typed

Since 2026-11-11 an earth wire's current may be **typed instead of
computed** (a measured or a specified current, e.g. a share of a net
current returning in it): magnitude and angle, along the line, the same
in every span of its section. It is carried whether the induced currents
are on or off. With them on, the typed earth wires are given currents
beside the phases': the others follow from

\\[ \underline I_f = -\,Z_{ff}^{-1}\left( Z_{fp}\,\underline I_p + Z_{ft}\,\underline I_t \right) \\]

(\\( f \\) the earth wires computed, \\( t \\) those typed, \\( p \\) the
phases; the same primitive matrices). Not together with the structures'
network (it computes every earth wire's current: refused). **Neither
conservative nor not**: the typed value is the user's. Tested
(`an_earth_wire_current_typed`): typed at the induced value it gives the
induced field to \\( 10^{-9} \\); 60 A alone give \\( \mu_0 \cdot 60 /
(2\pi \cdot 500) = 0.024 \\) µT at 500 m.

## The ground's return currents — left out

**Simplification.** The earth's own currents (eddy currents, and the
return of a net current such as an earth wire's) are not modelled: the
field is that of the wires' currents in free space. Their effect can be
estimated with the complex depth (Deri, Tevan, Semlyen, Castanheira,
"The complex ground return plane", *IEEE Trans. PAS-100* (8), 1981): the
earth acts like an image of each current at the depth
\\( h + 2\underline p \\), \\( \underline p = \sqrt{\rho/(j\omega\mu_0)} \\).
At 50 Hz and 100 Ω·m, \\( |\underline p| \approx 503 \\) m: the images lie
about 1 km down.

- **Balanced phases** (the currents sum to zero): the images form a
  balanced set about 1 km away, whose field falls as \\( 1/d^2 \\): for
  1000 A on a flat line of 8 m spacing,
  \\( \sqrt3\,\mu_0 I s/(2\pi d^2) \approx 0.003 \\) µT at the ground.
- **A net current**, e.g. 100 A in an earth wire returning through the
  earth: \\( \mu_0 I/(2\pi d) \approx 2\cdot10^{-7}\cdot 100/1000 \approx 0.02 \\) µT.
- At 10 Ω·m (\\( |\underline p| \approx 160 \\) m) about three times
  that.

Small against fields of the order of 1 µT; larger in relative terms far
from the line, where the field is small anyway (the guide notes the
model's uncertainty grows well below 1 µT, 8.1.4). Neither conservative
nor not in general (the images oppose their sources' near fields).

## Where

![Three ways to see the field: a profile, a row of points at a height above the ground with the field plotted along it; a cut, a grid of points in a vertical plane with isolines; a volume, a lattice in a box with surfaces around the wires](../images/theory/field-views.svg)

- **Profiles** across the line at stations (every span's lowest point by
  default — the lowest point of its lowest phase conductor — or a place
  in a span), square to the span's axis, ±100 m every 1 m, at a height
  above the ground (1 m by default). The ground — *Ground from*
 : the surface of the checks, a terrain surface (TIN layer)
  of the project, or the survey's ground points along the spans made into
  a surface for the run; without any, straight between the structures'
  feet and level across (**Simplification**).
- **What a run makes**: **2D** — the cuts asked and, asked, the profiles and
  points; **3D** — the volumes only. Each kind is a step of its own, one
  after another: the cuts and the volumes from the wires in 3D, the
  profiles by their model (3D or 2D). The wires from the window's weather
  case and calculation, or a system's own (*Per electrical system*: e.g.
  the conductors at their load's temperature, the earth wires at the
  air's); each case and calculation solved once, every section taken from
  its system's.
- **Grids** on a horizontal plane at that height over a span, their own
  step (`grid_step`): the levels' isolines from above — in the engine and
  `ohl`; the window no longer offers them.
- **Vertical cuts across the line**: a grid in a vertical
  plane, ± the width, from the lowest ground under it to some metres over
  the highest wire, every step; every point computed (also under the
  ground; the drawing stops at the ground, straight between the
  columns). **The plane**: at a station of an alignment,
  vertical and perpendicular to the alignment there; within 1 cm of an
  alignment point (an angle point) along the **bisector** of the two
  directions, as a structure stands — the same placement as a
  cross-section panel (one implementation, `ohl_line::route::plane_at`),
  so the run's cut and the panel opened at its station are one plane.
  A place in a span (its middle, its lowest point, a distance from its
  first structure) is first turned into the alignment station of that
  place, between the stations of the span's structures. Where the
  alignment and the span's chord are not parallel (a span beside the
  alignment, or the bisector at an angle point) the plane crosses the
  wires obliquely: the field is computed in that plane all the same (3D,
  the wires as solved), and a cut through an angle point shows both
  spans' wires as they meet at the structure. A span on no alignment
  can have no cut. The ground under the cut: the terrain where given,
  else straight between the feet of the span the plane crosses at the
  axis. The cross-section panels' own cuts (computed whenever a panel
  opens) take the panel's plane as it is.
- **Volumes**: the isosurfaces of the levels in a box over a
  span (± the width, from the lowest ground under it to some metres over
  the highest wire), see below. Where the boxes of two volumes overlap
  (two alignments side by side, a branch leaving a structure), the
  coarse cells lying wholly in the volume computed first are left to
  it: the later volume does not refine them and draws no surface in
  them, so each place is computed and drawn once (2026-11-10). Every
  value drawn is computed as before; only which lattice carries the
  surface there changes, and at the first box's edge the two surfaces
  overlap by at most one coarse cell (four steps) and may show a seam,
  a matter of the drawing alone.
- **Given points** (e.g. places of sensitive use) from a CSV file: x, y and
  an elevation or a height above the ground.

![In plan: an alignment with an angle point and the wires along it; a cut at a station perpendicular to the alignment, a cut at the angle point along the bisector, a profile square to its span's axis](../images/theory/field-cut-plane.svg)

![A double-circuit line in a vertical cut across it with a house beside it: the grid's points, the isolines of 3, 10 and 30 microtesla cut at the ground; the 3 microtesla line reaches the house](../images/theory/field-cut.svg)

With an optional **limit** (µT, yours to give): the margin at each point
(limit − B) and per profile the extent where B is at or above it (the
limit line's width at that height), and whether it reaches beyond the
profile.

## Cuts, volumes and what they are drawn with

Every value at a grid or lattice point is the field computed in full;
**only the drawing between the points is interpolated** (owner,
2026-10-18: speed may only coarsen the display, never computed
precision; the wires modelled are the user's choice, with its effect
measured):

- **Isolines** (marching squares, the two-dimensional case of W. E. Lorensen and H. E. Cline, "Marching cubes: a high resolution 3D surface construction algorithm", *Computer Graphics* (SIGGRAPH '87) 21 (4), 1987, pp. 163–169): where a level crosses a cell's edge,
  placed by the logarithm of the two values (a field falls about as a
  power of the distance, so its logarithm is closer to straight between
  points), straight across the cell; a saddle decided by the cell's
  mean.
- **Isosurfaces** (marching tetrahedra, A. Doi and A. Koide, "An efficient method of triangulating equi-valued surfaces by using tetrahedral cells", *IEICE Transactions on Information and Systems* E74-D (1), 1991, pp. 214–224: each lattice cube cut into six
  tetrahedra around its diagonal, so neighbouring cubes split their
  faces alike and the surface has no cracks), the crossings placed as
  above.
- **The volume's lattice** is computed only where a surface can pass: a
  coarse lattice first (4 steps apart), then the fine one in every coarse
  cell where a level lies between its corners' values or within 10 % of
  them (a surface bulging into the cell), in every cell within a coarse
  step of a wire piece (where a thin surface around a conductor can hide
  between coarse points), and, again and again, in every cell beyond a
  face of a fine cell that a level crosses (the surface is followed until
  it closes or leaves the box; tested: no holes but at the box's sides).
  **In parts**: a long volume's lattice is walked along its
  run in parts of whole coarse cells, at most some 20 million points
  (160 MB) held at once; two parts share the plane where they meet, the
  next starting from the values the one before computed there and
  following every surface that crosses it — the same points computed and
  the same surfaces as in one piece (tested). A surface that would come
  back across a shared plane into the part before after leaving it is
  only followed forward (**Simplification**: a fold of a surface back
  along the run, which the closed tubes around the wires do not make).
  **Simplification**: a surface lying wholly between coarse points, away
  from every wire and more than 10 % off the values around it, is not
  found — it would be smaller than a coarse cell. On a real span about
  half the fine lattice was computed. **Thin pieces left out**
 : a connected piece of a surface at most 2 steps across in
  two directions and at most 20 steps long is a stretch of a tube thinner
  than the lattice (round an earth wire at 5 kV/m: centimetres), met only
  where a lattice point happens to lie inside it; drawn, such pieces were
  beads along the wire (2–3 m thick, 4–21 m long at 2 m steps on a real
  line; the surface round the conductors 650 m). They are left out of the
  drawing and counted with each volume — a display choice, the values
  computed unchanged; a finer step resolves the tube.

  ![A slice of a volume's lattice: the coarse points, the coarse cells computed fine because a level passes near their corners' values, because a wire is near, or because a surface crosses into them; the levels' surfaces around three wires](../images/theory/field-volume.svg)

- **How far the drawing lies off its level is measured**, not assumed:
  after a run the field is computed again at up to 200 points spread over
  every isoline and surface, and the relative difference to the level
  reported (half of the points within …, nine in ten within …, the
  largest). It is largest where the field bends sharply between points,
  near the conductors: on a real line (1000 A per circuit) the 1 µT
  surface lay within 0.05 % for nine points in ten at a 2 m step, the
  10 µT one within 1 %, the 100 µT one (a tube of some 2 m around each
  conductor) within 15 % and up to 55 %; at 1 m within 0.01 %, 0.3 % and
  4 % (up to 11 %).
- **The wires modelled** (`modelled_within`, optional; the default is the
  whole line): a cut or volume may take the wires within a distance only
  (a piece by its nearer end and half its length). **Simplification,
  measured**: the whole line's field is computed at up to 200 points of
  the cut or volume and the relative difference reported. On a real line
  of 28 km, 2 km left the field within 0.03 % (500 m: 0.6 %).
- **Time.** The cost is the points times the pieces (some ns each per
  core). The estimates in the window take the last run's speed on the
  computer, else a short measurement halved (see *Performance* below).

## Cut-offs and grids (measured)

(Owner: "give indications on the cutoffs and the improvement
with better grids … keep it quick enough".) Every value at a point is
computed in full, from every piece of every wire summed; what a setting
changes is **which wires are summed** (the cut-off) or **where the
points are** (the grids), and from that how a drawing follows the
field. Measured on the example double circuit
(`examples/electrical`, two spans, 800 A per circuit;
`crates/ohl-electrical/tests/sensitivity.rs`, a release build).

**The cut-off: the wires modelled within a distance.** Only the pieces
within *d* of a cut or volume are summed; each run measures, at some 200
of the cut's points, how much the pieces left out change it.

![In plan, five spans and a cut across the middle one; the wires within d of the cut summed, those beyond left out](../images/theory/field-cutoff.svg)

| Modelled within | Pieces summed | The cut against the whole line (nine in ten / largest) | Time of the cut |
|---|---|---|---|
| 25 m | 72 | 50 % / 55 % | 2 ms |
| 50 m | 144 | 23 % / 28 % | 3 ms |
| 100 m | 264 | 7.3 % / 9.2 % | 5 ms |
| 200 m | 444 | 1.2 % / 1.6 % | 8 ms |
| the whole line (default) | 768 | — | 12 ms |

The field of a long straight conductor falls off slowly (as 1/r), and a
line's phases nearly cancel only far away: a short cut-off leaves out
much. On a 28 km line, 2 km left the cuts within 0.03 % and made a
volume ten times quicker — the cut-off is for long lines, at a distance
of a kilometre or more, and each run says what it changed. **2 km is the
window's default** (the defaults reviewed for accuracy
and speed: 0.03 % for ten times the speed); a line shorter than that is
modelled whole all the same.

**On a real line**: a 27 km 220 kV line of five circuits
(1000 A in each, assumed), 34 cuts along it — every kilometre and one
at each line angle over 25°, ±150 m every 1 m — against the whole line.
The points of 1 µT or more:

| Modelled within | Largest difference (B ≥ 1 µT) | Largest (B ≥ 0.1 µT) | The 1 µT isoline 1 m above the ground | Time (34 cuts) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 500 m | 1.4 % | 9.0 % | one grid step (1 m) off at 9 cuts | 6.6 s |
| 1 km | 1.0 % | 7.8 % | 1 m off at 4 cuts | 7.4 s |
| **2 km (default)** | **0.16 %** | **1.4 %** | **1 m off at 3 cuts** | **9.0 s** |
| 5 km | 0.02 % | 0.15 % | 1 m off at 1 cut | 13.4 s |
| the whole line | — | — | — | 27 s |

The shorter cut-offs fail where the line bends or another line comes
near (the largest differences at an angle and near a substation the line
shares with another); at 2 km the 1 µT isoline moves only where it sits
on its level within a grid step, and the 1.4 % lies in the faint field
below 1 µT (some 0.002 µT). So the 2 km default holds, for a third of
the time.

**The width.** On the same line the 1 µT isoline, 1 m above the ground,
lay 52 to 76 m from the axis on one side in most cuts and 90 m in the
cut along the bisector of a 49° angle: at the former ±50 m it reached
the edge of 30 of the 34 cuts, cut short. **Profiles, cuts and volumes
reach ±100 m by default**: the 34 cuts took 7.9 s
against 6.1 s at ±50 m (the project's reading and the wires' solving
included). Volumes twice as wide take about twice as long. Where a higher current or a lower level reaches farther, widen
it: a level that touches the edge of a cut is cut short there. The
electric field keeps ±50 m (its limits lie near the wires; its floors
grow with the width).

**A cut's step.** The field at every grid point is exact; an isoline is
drawn straight between the points where it crosses a cell, so it cuts
the corners where it bends tightly — near the wires, where the higher
levels' isolines are small circles.

![Left: a coarse grid around a wire, the isoline drawn straight between its crossings, cutting the circle's corners; right: a fine grid following the circle](../images/theory/field-grid-step.svg)

| Step | Points (±60 m) | 1 µT | 3 µT | 10 µT | 30 µT | Time |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0.25 m | 96 681 | 0.00 % | 0.00 % | 0.01 / 0.40 % | 0.09 / 0.16 % | 49 ms |
| 0.5 m | 24 341 | 0.00 % | 0.01 % | 0.46 / 1.5 % | 0.33 / 0.61 % | 12 ms |
| **1 m (default)** | 6 171 | 0.01 % | 0.03 % | 1.0 / 6.8 % | 1.3 / 2.9 % | 3 ms |
| 2 m | 1 586 | 0.03 % | 0.12 % | 5.0 / 32 % | 2.9 / 10 % | 1 ms |

(The isolines' points off their level: nine in ten / the largest.) The
low levels — those of the limits at the ground and at houses, 1 µT for
the NISV's installation limit — are wide and gentle: a 1 m grid draws
them within 0.03 %, at a quarter of the points of 0.5 m. Close to the
wires a finer step is needed for the higher levels; set it where those
matter. **Quicker at the same accuracy where it matters**: more cuts at
1 m instead of fewer at 0.5 m.

**A volume's step.** The surfaces' points off their level (nine in ten
/ largest), the lattice points computed of the whole, over one span ±40 m:

| Step | Points computed | 1 µT | 3 µT | 10 µT | 30 µT | Time |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 4 m | 27.5 k of 27.5 k | 0.26 / 0.52 % | 0.59 / 1.2 % | 4.0 / 38 % | 19 / 49 % | 0.03 s |
| **2 m (default)** | 160 k of 182 k | 0.08 / 0.19 % | 0.17 / 0.28 % | 1.0 / 30 % | 5.2 / 11 % | 0.15 s |
| 1 m | 804 k of 1 292 k | 0.03 / 0.04 % | 0.04 / 0.08 % | 0.22 / 10 % | 1.3 / 2.6 % | 0.73 s |

As for the cuts, the wide low levels are drawn closely at 2 m; the
tubes of the high levels around each conductor want 1 m or less.

**Long volumes in parts** keep the memory low without changing a value:
see [Cuts, volumes and what they are drawn with](#cuts-volumes-and-what-they-are-drawn-with).

## The choices and why

| Choice | Why | Effect on the result |
|---|---|---|
| Balanced currents by default, no harmonics | the governing state the NISV guide describes (2.4.1, 8.1.4) | near the line neither in general (**Assumption**); **NOT ON THE SAFE SIDE far from the line** (no net current), said in the result — give the currents per phase to include one |
| The wires in free space (no earth currents, no steel's permeability) | both are small at the profiles' distances (estimated above) | neither in general; small |
| 3D by default | it sees the sag, line angles, the line's length, jumpers | none; 2D under the lowest point is **conservative** against 3D on sagging spans |
| 2D at the wires' place in the profile (not the mean height) | the field is largest where the wires hang lowest | **conservative** against the mean height |
| The jumpers on by default | they carry the current through the structures | off: **not conservative** near the structures |
| Earth wire currents off by default | they need the earthing resistances; their sign depends on the phasing | either sign: run both when it matters |
| Through the structures' earthing (when the earth wires are on) | the infinitely long earth wire overstates the currents on a short line by far | neither in general; the line's ends **not conservative** (no substation earthing) |
| 10 Ω when no earthing resistance is given | a common design target, said | neither |
| The whole line modelled by default | quality before speed; leaving wires out changes values (measured) | none; *wires within* is the user's choice, its effect said |
| Only the drawing between points interpolated | speed may only coarsen the display, never computed precision | the drawing's offset from its levels measured and said |

## What is left out

- **The earth's currents** (eddy currents, the return of a net current):
  [estimated above](#the-grounds-return-currents--left-out), small.
- **The steel's magnetic permeability** (structures, earth wire cores):
  estimated above, small beyond a few metres.
- **Unbalance**, unless the currents are given per phase: balanced input
  holds no net current — **not on the safe side far from the line**
  ([above](#balanced-input-and-the-net-current)), said in the result.
- **Harmonics**: the currents are at power frequency.
- **A substation's earthing grid** at the line's ends (with the earth
  wire network): **not conservative** near the ends.
- **Other lines** (crossings, parallel lines): not in the model yet
  (said).
- **DC systems**: a static field (said).
- **The modelled length** beyond the line's ends: warned where the line is
  shorter than five times the limit line's reach on either side.
